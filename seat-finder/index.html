What’s iSEE?

Find your way to your seat

iSEE is designed so everyone can find their seat in the way that suits them: reading, listening, step by step or as an overview, with or without stairs. There is no diagnosis to give, no account to make and nothing to explain.

iSEE is a route planner by Autism & Me. Choose your seat, say where you are, and follow plain directions that use things already in the building: entrances, signs, lifts, row markers and seat numbers.

Try the demo How it works

There are no live venues yet. The demo uses a made-up theatre.

  • No beacons. Nothing is installed in the building.
  • No new markers. Directions point to doors, signs, lifts, concourses, row markers and seat numbers that are already there.
  • Text at every step. Every step is written in plain words. A map is optional.

Walkthrough

A big building can be hard to read before you get there. iSEE lets you preview the way to your seat, from the front door to your row. You see what you will pass, what to look for, and where the stairs and lifts are.

You choose how to follow it: one step at a time, or as an overview. Read it, or have a step read aloud when you ask. You can go back, repeat a step, or say you are somewhere else.

How to use

1. Choose your seat

Enter the section, row and seat number from your ticket. iSEE checks the seat against the venue's seating plan.

2. Choose where you are

Pick the entrance or place you are starting from. Each choice shows the sign to look for.

3. Follow the route

One step at a time, in plain words, with the sign or landmark to look for. You confirm each checkpoint.

System

iSEE works with the building as it is. The venue describes its checkpoints and the paths between them. Your phone works out the route. You confirm each checkpoint as you reach it.

4 steps to set up a venue

  1. Share approved floor and seating plans.
  2. Map existing landmarks, entrances, lifts and rows.
  3. Check routes and step-free status on site.
  4. Publish after the venue approves.

No beacons to install.

The same diagram in words

  1. Venue map data. The venue is described as checkpoints and the paths between them. Each checkpoint names a sign, lift, door or row marker that is already in the building.
  2. Phone. iSEE finds the shortest path that is allowed, from where you are to your seat, and shows it as one written step at a time. Sensors would be used only in future phone apps.
  3. You. You confirm each checkpoint as you reach it. You choose the start point and whether the route must be step-free. Confirming works both ways: the phone shows the next step, and you tell it where you are.

A short engineering explanation

  • A venue is a directed graph. Checkpoints are the points. Paths are the one-way lines between them. Each path has its own instruction, because "turn left at the clock" only works in one direction.
  • Routing finds the shortest path. Stairs, lifts, doors and aisles each have a length or a stated cost.
  • Step-free mode never uses stairs. It also never uses a path that has not been checked. Unknown is not treated as step-free.
  • Closed paths are skipped. If a closure appears while you are on a route, iSEE offers to work out a new route. It does not change your route without asking.
  • You confirm each checkpoint. In the web demo, that is how iSEE knows where you are. This website does not use your phone's sensors.
  • The planned phone apps would use the phone's own sensors to help follow your progress. Confirming checkpoints by hand would always stay available. The apps are not available and have not been tested on phones or in venues.
